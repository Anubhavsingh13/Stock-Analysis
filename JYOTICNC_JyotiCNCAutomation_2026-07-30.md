## Executive Summary
Jyoti CNC is India's largest listed pure-play metal-cutting CNC machine-tool maker and the domestic leader in simultaneous 5-axis machines (~10% share), riding a powerful aerospace/defence + China+1 + domestic-manufacturing capex tailwind, with a ₹4,732 Cr order book (~2.3× FY26 sales) and capacity nearly tripling from 6,000 to 16,000 machines/year by H2 FY27. The thesis risk is threefold: (1) the business is deeply cyclical — it posted three straight years of losses (FY20–22) in the last downcycle and carries very high operating leverage; (2) cash conversion is poor — reported PAT of ₹316–336 Cr has come with negative free cash flow every year FY24–26 owing to a ~210-day operating cycle and heavy capex; and (3) at ~46× standalone / ~54× consolidated trailing earnings and ~9× book, the price already prices in near-flawless execution, leaving no margin of safety despite a 45% de-rating from the ₹1,056 peak. Great franchise, demanding price, hostile chart — a watchlist name, not a buy here.

## BUSINESS PRIMER

Jyoti CNC Automation makes computer-numerically-controlled (CNC) machine tools — the "mother machines" that cut, turn, mill and shape metal into precision components. Its range spans CNC lathes/turning centres, 3-axis and simultaneous 5-axis machining centres, special-purpose and gear-cutting machines, sold overwhelmingly B2B to manufacturers in aerospace & defence, auto & auto-components, general engineering, EMS, dies & moulds, medical devices and the diamond/jewellery trade. Revenue is essentially **volume × realisation**: it shipped ~5,550 machines in FY26 at a blended realisation near ₹36–38 lakh, with realisation rising as mix shifts to high-end 5-axis and aerospace machines. Manufacturing is concentrated at Rajkot, Gujarat (with a captive foundry for castings and a new 20-acre plot at Tumakuru, Karnataka for future expansion), plus the acquired French subsidiary **Huron Graffenstaden** which gives it high-end European engineering credibility and export reach. The two things the business absolutely depends on: a live customer capex cycle (its orders are its customers' capital spending) and access to imported controllers — the "brain" of every machine — sourced from Fanuc (Japan) and Siemens (Germany) with 12-month lead times.

This is a **cyclical, working-capital-heavy capital-goods manufacturer** — not a capital-light compounder. Its earnings are a geared bet on India's (and increasingly the world's) manufacturing-investment cycle: demand can swing violently, as the FY20–22 losses demonstrate. The key value drivers are order inflow (volume) and mix-led realisation/margin; the key risks are the capex cycle turning, a ~210-day cash-conversion cycle that swallows cash during growth, dependence on foreign controller suppliers, and ~26% overseas revenue that carries FX and (via Huron) export-control risk. Two structural features colour the whole analysis: (a) the company only listed in **January 2024** (₹1,000 Cr fresh IPO, ₹475 Cr used to repay debt), so the public track record and governance history are short; and (b) the reported profit boom of FY24–26 sits on top of a decade that includes a near-death downturn — the moat and the margins must be judged against that full cycle, not just the up-leg.

## MODULE 1b — Sector Cycle: Tailwinds & Headwinds

**Secular vs. Cyclical demand decomposition (the single most important step here):**

Jyoti's FY24–26 revenue trebled off the FY20–22 trough (₹532 Cr FY21 → ₹2,093 Cr FY26). This growth is a **blend**:
- **Secular (durable, ~10–14% baseline):** India's manufacturing formalisation, defence indigenisation (positive-indigenisation lists, aerospace offsets), China+1 tool-room reshoring, PLI-driven electronics/EMS capacity, and rising 5-axis penetration from a low base. Aerospace/defence is now 38–39% of orders/revenue and is genuinely multi-year.
- **Cyclical (temporary, mean-reverts, ~10–15% of the recent growth rate):** the current pan-Indian private + PSU capex upcycle and post-COVID machine-tool restocking. Machine-tool demand is a leading-geared function of customers' own capex — it overshoots in upcycles and collapses in downcycles.

**Conclusion:** ~10–14% of demand growth is structural and should anchor the DCF mid-period; growth above ~15% embeds a live capex upcycle that will fade within 2–3 years. **Do not extrapolate the 25–35% recent CAGR into perpetuity.**

**Other inputs:**
- **Input cost cycle:** steel/casting and imported controllers/electronics; largely stable-to-favourable now, but controller supply (Fanuc/Siemens, 12-mo lead) is a structural bottleneck and a pricing lever for suppliers.
- **Inventory cycle:** company runs enormous WIP/RM inventory (RM 228 days, WIP 112 days FY25) by design (long build + import lead times), so it is less exposed to channel destocking but more exposed to demand air-pockets crystallising as dead inventory.
- **Competitive capacity pipeline:** Jyoti is adding ~10,000 machines of capacity; global majors (DMG Mori, Yamazaki Mazak, Haas, Hurco, Doosan) and Chinese/Taiwanese entrants are also expanding in/into India. Simultaneous capacity additions raise the medium-term risk of pricing pressure in the mid-range, less so in high-end 5-axis/aerospace.
- **Geopolitical map:** (1) Export markets — Huron export-licence investigation and US tariff overhang; (2) sourcing — critical dependence on Japan/Germany controllers (structural, not re-routable in a quarter); (3) FX — ~26% overseas revenue.

**Net FX exposure:** ~26% revenue overseas partly offset by imported controller/component costs; company uses forward hedges. Net exposure modest but real; a sharp INR move swings a few percent of EBIT.

**Sector cycle verdict: Tailwind (structural in A&D) overlaid on a Cyclical upcycle (fading).**
Primary driver: defence/aerospace indigenisation + manufacturing capex — multi-year on the structural leg, but the cyclical leg is late-ish and should not be embedded as permanent. **Downstream: widen MoS; anchor DCF to ~12% durable growth, not 30%.**

## MODULE 1c — Stock Cycle: Company-Specific Positioning

**Revenue-growth trajectory (YoY, consolidated):** FY24 +44%, FY25 +36%, FY26 **+15%** — clearly **decelerating** at the top line, and consolidated PAT grew only ~**+6%** in FY26 (₹316→₹336 Cr) as Huron's export-licence deferment (₹67 Cr revenue) and rising interest/depreciation bit. This deceleration against elevated expectations is the proximate cause of the 45% share de-rating.

**Revenue decomposition — Volume / Price / Mix:** Recent growth is **mix-led** (most durable): units actually *dipped* standalone (3,600 FY24 → 3,517 FY25) while realisation jumped ₹0.33 Cr → ₹0.46 Cr on a shift to high-end 5-axis/aerospace machines. Consolidated volumes then rose to ~5,550 in FY26. The next leg must come from **volume** (the 16,000-machine capacity) — which is bounded by demand filling the new capacity and by the WC needed to build it.

**Physical capacity model:**
- Installed capacity: 5,000 → 6,000 (end-FY25) → **16,000 machines/yr by H2 FY27** (COD ~Oct 2026; project cost ₹425.83 Cr; ₹300 Cr Union Bank term loan + ₹125.83 Cr internal accruals; 90% civil / 80% machinery done by Dec-25). New capacity ramps ~50% initial utilisation with a targeted 20–30% throughput uplift.
- Facilities ran **>100% utilisation** in peak FY26 months — a genuine constraint that the expansion relieves.
- **Capacity ceiling:** at ~11,000–12,000 machines (≈70–75% of 16,000), volume growth stalls unless mix/pricing or the Tumakuru phase adds more. This underpins the ~₹3,700–4,000 Cr revenue ceiling by FY29 (see Module 10).

**Operating leverage:** high. DOL is elevated (fixed foundry/plant base + long build cycle) — evidenced by the FY20–22 loss years on modest revenue declines. DFL is now low (deleveraged post-IPO, ICR ~12× FY26). **DTL remains high through DOL**, so a demand miss hits EPS hard.

**Management guidance accuracy:** short history (listed Jan-24) but broadly delivered — margin guidance (25%+ EBITDA), capacity milestones and order-book growth have tracked. One recurring slippage risk: capacity commissioning timelines (COD pushed toward Oct-26/H2 FY27). WTT ≈ MODERATE (see Module 4).

**FCF inflection year:** **FY28–29** — the point where the ₹426 Cr capacity project completes (maintenance-only capex) *and* the WC-to-revenue ratio stabilises. Until then FCF stays constrained/negative.

**Stock-cycle classification: Early-to-mid cycle on the business (capex-heavy ramp, revenue accelerating on capacity) but late-cycle on the sector demand + late-stage on the share price.** MoS adjustment: standard-to-wider; position size: normal only if bought with a real discount.

## MODULE 3 — Business Quality & Moat

### 3A. Greenwald's Three Barriers
1. **Supply/Cost advantage — Partial.** Captive foundry, Rajkot cluster economics and scale give a domestic cost edge, but the value-defining controller is bought-in (no proprietary control system like Fanuc/Siemens/Heidenhain), capping the cost moat.
2. **Customer captivity — Moderate, and improving.** Aerospace/defence qualification cycles are long and switching costs high once a machine is designed into a customer's process; repeat orders from marquee clients. This is the strongest moat leg and is growing (A&D now 38–39%).
3. **Local economies of scale — Yes, domestically.** #1 listed Indian pure-play, ~10% share of India's 5-axis market, largest domestic footprint — a scale/service-density advantage within India.

### 2B. Moat durability
- **ROIC > WACC:** Yes *now* (ROCE ~21% vs WACC ~13.5%), but **only since FY23** — negative through FY20–22. Not yet a full-cycle proof.
- **Market-share stability:** gaining share in a growing 5-axis market; too short a listed history to certify ±3% stability.
- **Pricing power:** demonstrated via mix-led realisation gains (₹0.33→0.46 Cr) and 28%+ standalone EBITDA — genuine, but partly cyclical.
- **Activity system:** foundry + design + assembly + service is reinforcing and hard to replicate domestically at scale.

**Moat verdict: NARROW-to-MODERATE.** Real and widening in high-end A&D; capped by controller dependence and cyclicality.

## MODULE 7 — Market Size & Market Share

- **TAM:** India's machine-tool consumption is ~₹25,000–30,000 Cr and India is among the world's top consumers but a large *net importer* (China/Japan/Taiwan/Germany supply a big share) — so the **import-substitution + China+1 opportunity is the core structural TAM driver.** Global machine-tool market is ~$90–100B. India CNC/5-axis is growing double-digits off a low base.
